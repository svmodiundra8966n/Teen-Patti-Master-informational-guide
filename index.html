The Six Hand Rankings in Teen Patti Master

Most of the decisions you make in Teen Patti Master come down to one thing: knowing which hands beat which. On most tables there are six categories, and they always rank in the same order. Once you remember this order, you stop guessing and start folding the hands that are not worth playing.

Trail

A Trail is three cards of the same rank. Three aces is the best hand in the game. Three twos is the weakest Trail, but it still beats every Pure Sequence, Sequence, Colour, Pair, and High Card. Trails do not come often. In a normal 52-card deck there are only 52 possible Trails out of 22,100 total three-card hands. That means roughly one Trail every 425 hands. When you get one, you are usually in a good spot, but you still have to decide how much to bet and whether to stay blind or look.

Pure Sequence

A Pure Sequence is three consecutive cards of the same suit. It is the second-best hand. A suited A-K-Q is typically the strongest Pure Sequence. A-2-3 is also a valid Pure Sequence on most tables, though its exact ranking relative to A-K-Q can vary. There are only 48 Pure Sequences in the deck, which makes them slightly rarer than Trails even though they rank lower. The reason Trails rank higher is tradition, not pure probability. Players who mix up Pure Sequence with a normal Sequence or with Colour lose chips unnecessarily. Remember: consecutive ranks and same suit together. Remove either condition and it is no longer a Pure Sequence.

Sequence

A Sequence is three consecutive ranks that are not all the same suit. It ranks below Pure Sequence and above Colour. The same ordering rules apply as with Pure Sequences: A-K-Q is usually highest, A-2-3 is valid, and the sequence descends to 4-3-2. There are 720 Sequences in the deck, so you will see them far more often than Trails or Pure Sequences. A Sequence beats any Colour, even a high Colour. That is one of the more common points of confusion for newer players.

Colour

Colour means three cards of the same suit that do not form a sequence. It ranks fourth. When two players both have Colour, compare the highest card first, then the second, then the third. A Colour with an Ace is stronger than a Colour with a King if the other cards do not change the order. Colour appears more often than Sequences, which is why Sequence ranks higher. Same suit alone is not enough; the cards must not be consecutive for it to count as Colour rather than Pure Sequence.

Pair

A Pair is two cards of the same rank plus one unmatched card. Pairs are common. There are more than 3,700 of them in the deck. Higher pairs beat lower pairs. If two players have the same pair rank, the third card (the kicker) decides. A pair of Queens with a King beats a pair of Queens with a Ten. Many rounds are won or lost on the strength of the pair and the quality of the kicker. Weak pairs are often better folded early than played for long.

High Card

If a hand does not form any of the five combinations above, it is a High Card hand. These make up the majority of all possible hands — roughly three out of every four. Compare the highest card first, then the next, then the last. An Ace-high hand is the strongest possible High Card, but it still loses to any Pair or better. Most of the skill in Teen Patti Master comes from knowing when a High Card or weak Pair is not worth continuing with.

When two hands fall into the same category, compare the highest relevant card first. Suits do not matter in the standard game. Exactly equal hands usually split or are resolved by the table’s specific tie rule. Always check the rules shown inside the Teen Patti Master app for the table you join, because some variants reverse the ranking or change how A-2-3 is ordered.

How a Round Works in Teen Patti Master

A standard round starts with the boot. Every player puts a small fixed amount into the pot before any cards are dealt. Then three cards are given face down to each player from a 52-card deck. No jokers are used in the classic version.

On your turn you choose whether to play blind or seen. Blind means you have not looked at your cards. Seen means you have looked. Blind players usually bet half of what seen players must put in. This creates a small cost advantage for staying blind early. Many experienced players stay blind while the pot is still small and only look once the pot grows or the betting becomes expensive.

The main actions are simple. You can match the current stake (often called chaal), raise the stake, or pack (fold). When only two players remain, either of them can request a show. The higher hand takes the entire pot. If more than two players are still in, a sideshow may be available between seen players under some rule sets. The exact options depend on the table settings in the app.

Rounds are short. That is part of the appeal. You do not need a long session to finish a hand. At the same time, the decisions about when to look, when to raise, and when to fold add up over many hands. Players who treat every hand as a must-win situation tend to lose chips faster than players who fold early and often.

Variants change the picture. Muflis reverses the rankings so the weakest hand wins. Joker and AK47 introduce wild cards. In those modes the classic order above does not apply. Always read the table rules before you join. Assuming classic rankings on a variant table is one of the quicker ways to lose chips.

Staying blind while the pot is still small costs less than looking immediately. The half-price betting structure gives you a temporary advantage. Once the pot grows large enough that the cost of continuing becomes significant, looking at your cards gives you better information for the next decision.

Common Mistakes on Teen Patti Master Tables

One frequent error is assuming the ranking order without checking the table. Muflis and other variants reverse or change the order. Joining a variant table with classic expectations costs chips quickly. Another common mistake is playing every hand. Because most hands are weak, selective folding improves results more than aggressive play on marginal cards.

Looking at cards too early is another habit that increases cost. Blind play is cheaper while the pot is small. Looking immediately removes that advantage for the rest of the hand. Players who look on the first turn often end up putting in more money than necessary on hands that should have been folded later anyway.

Ignoring the kicker on pairs is a smaller but steady leak. Two players with the same pair rank are decided by the third card. Treating all pairs of the same rank as equal leads to incorrect decisions when the kickers differ. The same logic applies to Colour and High Card hands: the order of the three cards matters once the category is the same.

Chasing losses is the mistake that compounds the others. After a few losing hands, some players increase their bet size or stay in longer than usual. That approach usually accelerates the loss rather than recovering it. The structure of the game already favours selective play. Adding emotional decisions on top of that makes the outcome worse.
